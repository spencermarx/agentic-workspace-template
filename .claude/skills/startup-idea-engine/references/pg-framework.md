# Reference: Paul Graham's "How to Get Startup Ideas" — Distilled

**Source:** [paulgraham.com/startupideas.html](https://www.paulgraham.com/startupideas.html) (2012)
**YC Library:** [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8z-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
**Status:** The most-cited startup ideation framework in the YC ecosystem. Foundational text for any structured startup ideation skill.

This reference is the distilled framework — load it instead of re-fetching the original essay each session. Update only when PG materially revises the essay or new YC framework guidance supersedes specific elements.

## The Three Pillars of a Well-Formed Idea

The very best startup ideas tend to have three things in common:
1. **Something the founders themselves want**
2. **Something the founders themselves can build**
3. **Something few others realize is worth doing**

All three must be present. Missing any one produces predictably weak ideas.

---

## The Demand-Shape Test: The Well, Not The Broad

Visualize demand as a graph: y-axis = how much each user wants it, x-axis = how many users.

- **The Well** (preferred): a small number of users want it desperately — so badly they'd use a crappy v1.
  - Microsoft's Altair Basic: thousands of users programming in machine language without it.
  - Facebook at Harvard: thousands of students who wanted it desperately.
- **The Broad but Shallow** (death): millions of users with mild interest.
  - Pet-owner social network. "Sum mild interest across the population and you have zero users."

**Operational test:** Who urgently needs this *right now* — so badly they'd use a crappy v1? If you can't name them with a face (title, company shape, situation, the specific moment of pain), the idea is probably bad.

## The Core Directive: Live in the Future and Build What's Missing

The single most important PG directive. Has three layers:

1. **Get to the leading edge** of a field that's changing fast — either as a practitioner pushing forward, or as an early adopter living with emerging technology.
2. **Look around for what's missing** that should obviously exist.
3. **Notice the gaps**, don't try to think them up.

The verb shift: **replace "think up" with "notice."**

Worked examples (PG's):
- Bill Gates and Paul Allen heard about Altair → prepared minds recognized opportunity.
- Drew Houston forgot USB stick → prepared mind saw file-sync gap.
- Mark Zuckerberg lived online → saw social publication as natural where others saw it as horrifying.

The pattern: **external stimulus hitting a prepared mind.** The preparation is doing things you find genuinely interesting at the leading edge of a field.

## Noticing vs. Thinking Up (The Critical Distinction)

**Thinking up ideas actively** produces: plausible-sounding but bad ideas — "sitcom ideas." They pass casual scrutiny. Friends say "maybe I'd use that." Real users don't show up.

**Noticing through living and building** produces: organic ideas grounded in real gaps. Requires maintaining a background process — "looking for things that seem to be missing." Work on hard problems out of curiosity; "have a second self watching over your shoulder."

Key insight: **the best founders didn't intend to start companies.** They noticed gaps while building what interested them.

## Authentication Signals (the idea is real, not hallucinated)

1. **Personal pain:** You experience the problem yourself. Skin in the game.
2. **The obvious-to-you test:** Once you find the right problem, you should be able to describe it as obvious — at least to you. If it's not obvious to you but you're at the leading edge, something's wrong.
3. **User desperation:** When you describe the unmet need, potential users recognize themselves urgently. Not "that could be useful" — "we need this immediately."
4. **The path-out indicator:** The narrow initial market has natural expansion routes (colleges → all colleges → everyone; Altair → other machines → operating systems).
5. **Competitive validation:** A crowded market is actually a good sign — it means demand exists and existing solutions aren't good enough. Your thesis must articulate what incumbents overlook.

---

## Warning Signs (the idea is hallucinated)

1. **The plausibility trap.** Ideas that sound reasonable on inspection are statistically more likely to be bad. "The set of plausible-sounding startup ideas is many times larger than the set of good ones." Good ideas often sound implausible initially.
2. **Sitcom / made-up ideas.** Invented by thinking, not noticing. A writer creating fiction would naturally produce these. They pass the friend test ("maybe I'd use that") without generating actual usage.
3. **Low activation energy.** People easily say they might use it but never do. Activation energy is too low → idea is too soft.
4. **Founder-customer gap.** You're building for "other people" who might want it eventually. PG's diagnostic question: "Would you use this if you hadn't written it?" Many founders answer no.

## The Six Generation Procedures (Plan B for when noticing doesn't produce)

PG positions these as Plan B; the skill `01-noticing.md` uses them as the primary mechanism for systematic generation.

1. **Start with your own needs.** "There must be things you need." What did you say at previous jobs: "Why doesn't someone make X?" People don't make such complaints about impossible things.
2. **Find your unusual position.** What makes you different? Age, previous experience, domain knowledge. Best signal: being young (teens / early twenties). Second-best: having worked on a different problem and noticed needs there.
3. **Talk to others about gaps.** Don't hunt explicitly for startup ideas. Ask what's tedious, annoying, missing in their work. Let conversations be general; spark thoughts rather than pitch.
4. **Become a consultant to a single user.** When you find unmet needs in others, act like you were retained to solve them. Build code for this one user; most becomes reusable. (Rajat Suri got a job as a waiter to understand restaurant problems.)
5. **Seek out unsexy / schlep-heavy problems.** Programming space is "stripped pretty clean" of convenient ideas. Move to messy, tedious problems; "valuable ones just sitting there." Stripe's whole opportunity was schlep-disabled founders — thousands knew payments were painful, but schlep blindness prevented them from building it.
6. **Ride waves.** Look for technological declines (Moore's law for gene sequencing, 3D printing). What becomes possible in 3-5 years that we currently rule out? What companies might profit from the decline of incumbents?

Plus: **Sell before build.** Paul Buchheit principle — "tell them to go sell the product ASAP." Trying to sell bad ideas reveals real ones discovered in the process.

---

## Filters to Disable

1. **The schlep filter (most dangerous).** Unconscious shrinking from tedious, messy, annoying work. Stripe benefited from disabled schlep filter; thousands saw the pain but flinched. Diagnosis: schlep blindness — you don't see the idea because you fear the work.
2. **The unsexy filter.** Prevents working on boring problems. Less dangerous than schlep filter because sex-appeal sometimes correlates with value — but can prevent good ideas.
3. **The "could this be a big company?" filter.** Turn off initially; apply later. Filtering for scale early eliminates good ideas. "Toy" classification is positive: "everything an idea needs except being important." Microcomputers, BackRub, Facebook all seemed trivial initially.
4. **The "obvious in retrospect" filter.** Accept that good ideas feel obvious once noticed but weren't visible before. Don't reject ideas because they seem obvious.

---

## Founder-Market Fit Indicators

1. **Domain expertise.** Only trust your judgment in domains where you have real knowledge. Prevents Dunning-Kruger mistakes.
2. **Living the future.** Your current lifestyle should already embody emerging patterns. Mark didn't need to research whether young people wanted social networks; he was one.
3. **The crossing-domains principle.** Expertise in one field applied to another produces ideas. Programming knowledge applied to biology reveals overlooked software solutions. New domain experts don't take status quo for granted.
4. **Previous startup as research.** Ideas discovered while solving other problems are especially promising. Hotmail began as a communication tool about founders' previous startup idea.

---

## The Final Meta-Framework

PG's closing principle: **"Live in the future and build what seems interesting."**

This replaces recipe-hunting because:
- Organic ideas feel like inspirations; forced ideas feel like ideas.
- You can't trust your judgment about ideas you manufactured.
- Best approach is indirect: develop the right background, then ideas seem obvious.
- Time and inclination to build things you find interesting matter more than methodology.

The framework succeeds through **becoming the sort of person who naturally notices gaps**, not through better ideation techniques.
